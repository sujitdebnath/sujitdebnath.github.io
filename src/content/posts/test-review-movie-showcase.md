_The Quiet Hour_ opens on a power outage and never really turns the lights
back on — and that turns out to be exactly the point. Ingrid Solberg's
third feature follows a night-shift hospital switchboard operator, Mara,
across a single blackout that strands her at her desk with nothing but a
dying phone line and the voices on the other end of it. It's a small,
patient film, and it earns every minute of that patience.

Solberg has always been interested in people whose jobs are to listen —
her 2015 debut was about a court stenographer — but _The Quiet Hour_
pushes the idea further by keeping Mara almost entirely alone on screen.
The film lives or dies on its sound design and on Noor Rahim's performance
as Mara, and both hold up. Rahim says more with the angle of her shoulders
than most actors manage with a page of dialogue.

What struck me most on a second viewing was how little the script
telegraphs. A caller mentions a name in passing in the first act; it
doesn't resurface until the last ten minutes, and when it does, the film
trusts you to remember it without underlining anything:

> "You said that name already. Twenty minutes ago, before the lights
> went. You don't remember saying it, do you?" - Sujit Debnath

<img src="/images/blog/eiffel-tower-2.jpg" alt="A dim hallway lit only by emergency signage — the kind of quiet, low-light framing the film returns to throughout" class="img-tall" />

The middle stretch drags slightly — there's a subplot involving a
maintenance worker that could have been trimmed by five minutes without
losing anything — but the film recovers fully by the final act, when Mara
finally says the thing she's been avoiding all night:

> "I'm not trying to fix it. I'm just trying to stay on the line until
> someone else picks up."

It's a small, unglamorous line, delivered without any music under it, and
it's the best thing in the movie.

If you liked the slow-burn, single-location tension of films like _Locke_
or _The Guilty_, _The Quiet Hour_ is worth seeking out — just go in
knowing it's more interested in what people say to strangers in the dark
than in any kind of plot mechanics. One more exchange has stuck with me
since the credits rolled:

> "Is anyone coming?"
> "I don't know. Stay on the line with me until they do."

Not a perfect film, but a genuinely felt one — and Rahim's performance
alone makes it worth the runtime.
